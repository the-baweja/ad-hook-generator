# Hook Framework Reference

Detailed descriptions, examples, and best-use-cases for each hook framework. Referenced from Step 4 of the Ad Hook Writer skill.

---

## Copy Hook Frameworks (Text-First)

These work as the first line of primary text in static ads, the opening line of a video voiceover, or the text overlay on the first frame.

### 1. Bold Claim
A declarative statement that challenges what the audience believes.
Example: "Your $60 moisturizer is doing nothing. Ours costs $24 and outperforms it clinically."
Best for: Product-aware audiences, competitive positioning.

### 2. Question
Opens with a question that creates an information gap the audience needs to close.
Example: "What if your skincare routine is actually making your acne worse?"
Best for: Problem-aware audiences, educational angles.

### 3. Statistic Lead
Leads with a surprising, specific number that stops the scroll because it contradicts expectations.
Example: "73% of women over 30 are using the wrong SPF. Here's how to check yours."
Best for: Educational angles, authority positioning.

### 4. "Did You Know..."
Classic information-gap opener. The ellipsis creates a micro-commitment to keep reading.
Example: "Did you know the 'clean beauty' label has zero FDA regulation?"
Best for: Problem-unaware audiences, myth-busting angles.

### 5. Testimonial Quote
Opens with a real customer's exact words â in quotes â so it reads as a person, not a brand.
Example: "'I cancelled my Sephora subscription after finding this.' â Jessica, 34"
Best for: Social proof angles, warm audiences. Strongest when the quote uses vivid, specific language from the Pain Point Miner.

### 6. Contrarian / Hot Take
Takes a position that goes against the mainstream advice in the category.
Example: "Stop using hyaluronic acid. Seriously. Here's why your dermatologist won't tell you this."
Best for: Scroll-stopping on cold traffic, thought-leader positioning.

### 7. "Stop Doing X"
Direct command that implies the reader is actively harming themselves. Creates urgency.
Example: "Stop layering 10 products every morning. Your skin barrier is begging you."
Best for: Problem-aware audiences who've tried everything.

### 8. Problem Callout
Names the exact pain point â preferably in the customer's own words from the Pain Point Miner.
Example: "Tired of foundation that oxidizes by noon? Yeah, we were too."
Best for: Problem-aware audiences, empathy-first angles.

### 9. "I Tried X and..."
First-person narrative opener. Reads like a real person sharing an experience, not a brand selling.
Example: "I tried 14 different serums last year. Only one actually did anything."
Best for: UGC-style angles, storytelling, warm audiences.

### 10. Social Proof Volume
Leads with the sheer scale of adoption â numbers that signal "everyone else already knows."
Example: "4,847 five-star reviews. Not paid. Not incentivized. Just honest."
Best for: Product-aware audiences, trust-building.

### 11. "The Truth About..."
Promises insider knowledge. Works because it implies a secret the audience hasn't been told.
Example: "The truth about 'medical grade' skincare (it's not what dermatologists want you to think)."
Best for: Problem-aware audiences, authority/education angles.

### 12. Before/After Statement
Contrasts the old state (pain) with the new state (transformation) in one line.
Example: "From cystic breakouts every month to clear skin for 8 months straight."
Best for: Transformation angles, result-driven audiences.

### 13. Curiosity Gap
Gives incomplete information that demands completion. The reader HAS to click "see more."
Example: "There's one ingredient in your moisturizer that's quietly destroying your skin barrier."
Best for: Cold traffic, awareness campaigns. Use sparingly â overuse feels clickbaity.

### 14. Demographic Callout
Calls out the exact person this ad is for. Acts as a targeting layer ON TOP of Meta's targeting.
Example: "If you're a woman over 40 who's given up on anti-aging products â read this."
Best for: Cold traffic, niche audiences. Dara Denney specifically recommends this for improving hook rate by filtering for the right audience.

### 15. "Nobody Talks About..."
Positions the hook as hidden knowledge. Flatters the reader â they're about to learn something exclusive.
Example: "Nobody talks about the real reason your pores look bigger in the afternoon."
Best for: Problem-unaware audiences, educational angles.

### 16. "Am I the Only One..."
Universal pain disguised as a personal question. Triggers instant recognition: "No, it's not just you."
Example: "Am I the only one whose concealer creases 20 minutes after applying it?"
Best for: Empathy angles, community-building. GOLD from the Pain Point Miner â pain points people think are personal but are actually universal.

### 17. Enemy / Villain
Names the thing that's working against the reader. Gives the pain point a face.
Example: "Big skincare companies spend millions making you think you need 12 products. You need 3."
Best for: Contrarian angles, founder-story angles, brand positioning.

### 18. Future Pacing
Puts the reader in the future where the problem is solved. They visualize the outcome before they know the product.
Example: "Imagine waking up, looking in the mirror, and actually liking what you see. No filter."
Best for: Aspirational angles, warm audiences.

### 19. Listicle
Number-driven hook that promises organized, scannable value. The number creates a concrete expectation.
Example: "5 things I stopped buying after working in the skincare industry for 10 years."
Best for: Educational angles, cold traffic. Odd numbers outperform even in testing.

### 20. Challenge
Dares the reader to try something. Creates a commitment micro-loop.
Example: "Try this for 7 days. If your skin doesn't look different, I'll refund you personally."
Best for: Offer angles, conversion campaigns, guarantee-led positioning.

## Visual Hook Frameworks (Video-First)

These define what happens in the first 0-3 seconds of a video ad â the visual, the audio, the text overlay. The visual hook and the copy hook work together. Organized into categories for easier matching.

---

### Category A: Performance & Product-Focused

#### 21. Product Demo Instant
Show the product working in the very first frame. No build-up, no intro. Result first.
Visual: Serum absorbing into skin, before/after in the first second, product in use.
Best for: Product-aware audiences, retargeting. Strongest when the product has a visually satisfying use moment.

#### 22. Unboxing
Anticipation, reveal, reaction. The oldest hook on social and still one of the highest-performing.
Visual: Hands opening package, tissue paper pulled back, product revealed.
Best for: New product launches, gifting angles, premium positioning.

#### 23. ASMR / Texture
Sensory-focused. The sound and visual of the product IS the hook.
Visual: Close-up of product texture, satisfying squeeze, crisp packaging sounds.
Best for: Beauty, food, physical products with satisfying textures. Extremely high hook rates.

#### 24. ASMR + Unboxing
Combines the anticipation of unboxing with the sensory satisfaction of ASMR. Every sound is intentional â the box opening, the tissue crinkling, the product clicking into place. No voiceover, no music. Pure sensory experience.
Visual: Macro close-ups of hands opening packaging in a quiet room. Each element revealed slowly with crisp audio.
Best for: Premium products, gifting, anything with high-quality packaging. The combination of anticipation + sensory satisfaction creates extremely high hook rates and hold rates. Works for both cold (the sounds stop the scroll) and warm (the product reveal converts).

#### 25. "Wait For It..."
Delayed payoff. The hook is the PROMISE that something satisfying is coming.
Visual: Process starting (mixing ingredients, applying product, timer counting).
Text overlay: "Wait for it..." or "Watch until the end"
Best for: Transformation, before/after, satisfying reveals. High hold rate when payoff delivers.

#### 26. Stop Motion
Product or objects animated frame-by-frame. Visually hypnotic because nothing in the feed looks like this. The movement pattern is so different from normal video that the brain can't ignore it.
Visual: Product pieces assembling themselves, ingredients walking into a blender, supplements lining up and disappearing one by one.
Best for: Cold traffic (pure pattern interrupt), educational (show ingredients combining), playful brands. Extremely high hook rates because of visual novelty, but production-intensive.

#### 27. Visual Pacifier Ads
Background visual that's oddly satisfying or calming â pouring, mixing, stacking, organizing â while text cards or voiceover deliver the selling message. The visual holds attention while the copy does the convincing. Named "pacifier" because the visual soothes the viewer into staying.
Visual: Satisfying loops â paint mixing, sand cutting, product being assembled, liquid pouring in slow motion. The visual doesn't need to be product-related.
Best for: Cold traffic where you need high hold rates. The satisfying visual keeps people watching while the text overlay or voiceover does the selling. Works for any product â the visual is the retention mechanism, not the message.

### Category B: Creator-Led / Talking Head

#### 28. Founder Direct-to-Camera
Founder speaks directly into camera. No production. Authenticity IS the hook.
Visual: Phone-quality, natural lighting, founder's face filling the frame.
Opening: "I'm [name], I started [brand], and I need to tell you something about [category]."
Best for: Founder-story angles, trust-building, warm audiences.

#### 29. Yap + Founder Story
Extended casual monologue where a creator or founder talks passionately about why the product exists, what problem they were trying to solve, or what they believe about the category. "Yapping" style â unscripted feel, authentic energy, no teleprompter vibes. The hook is the passion and conviction, not a polished opening line.
Visual: Creator sitting casually (couch, desk, car), phone-quality, mid-sentence energy as if continuing a conversation.
Opening: "Okay so I need to rant about this for a second..." or "Let me tell you why I spent 3 years on this..."
Best for: Founder stories, brand origin, mission-driven products. The long-form nature means lower hook rate but extremely high hold rate and conversion among those who stay.

#### 30. Walking & Talking
Creator walking somewhere while talking to camera. Movement + eye contact = attention.
Visual: Casual, not studio-lit. Movement in frame. Direct address.
Best for: UGC-style, founder stories, educational. Movement itself is a pattern interrupt.

#### 31. Ask Me Anything (AMA)
Q&A format. Creator answers real (or staged) questions from comments, DMs, or on-screen prompts. Each question is a mini-hook. "You asked, I'm answering."
Visual: Creator on camera with question appearing as text overlay. Can also show the actual comment/DM screenshot.
Opening: "Everyone keeps asking me this so let me just address it..." or "Your top 3 questions about [product], answered."
Best for: Objection handling (address real concerns), trust-building, warm audiences. Works especially well when the questions come from the Pain Point Miner â real language from real people.

#### 32. Review
Straightforward honest review format. Creator shares their experience after using the product for a specific time period. The hook is the verdict â give it in the first 3 seconds, then explain why.
Visual: Creator holding or next to the product, casual setting, direct address.
Opening: "I've been using [product] for 90 days. Here's my honest take." or "Everyone asked for my review. Let's do this."
Best for: Mid-funnel consideration. Works best when the reviewer is credible for the category and gives the verdict FIRST (not at the end).

### Category C: Multi-Person & Social

#### 33. Interview
Two-person format. One asks questions, one answers. Creates a conversational dynamic that feels more authentic than a monologue. The interviewer acts as the audience's proxy â asking what they're thinking.
Visual: Two people in frame (or cut between them). Casual setting. The interviewee is the expert or customer.
Opening: "So tell me honestly â does this actually work?" or "What made you start [brand]?"
Best for: Founder stories (interviewer asks the origin story), customer testimonials (interviewer guides the story), expert endorsements. The back-and-forth creates natural engagement.

#### 34. Interview on the Street
Man-on-the-street style. Random people (or seeming random) are asked to try the product, answer a question, or give their opinion. The unscripted reactions are the hook.
Visual: Public setting (sidewalk, park, mall), handheld camera, multiple quick cuts between different people's reactions.
Opening: "We asked 50 strangers to try [product] blind. Watch their reactions." or "What do you think this costs?" (holds up product)
Best for: Cold traffic, social proof at scale (many people = many data points), taste tests, price perception experiments. The authenticity of stranger reactions outperforms curated testimonials.

#### 35. Skit Ads
Mini comedy/drama scene that plays out a relatable scenario. The product is the punchline or the resolution. Story structure: setup (relatable problem) â escalation (make it worse/funnier) â resolution (product saves the day). The hook is the scenario recognition â viewer sees themselves in the first 2 seconds.
Visual: Two or more people acting out a scene. Slightly overacted for comedy. Quick cuts. TikTok native feel.
Example: "When your friend who takes 15 supplements every morning sees you drink one glass and leave..." (friend's jaw drops)
Best for: Cold traffic (entertainment value stops the scroll), younger demographics, brands with personality. Extremely shareable â the comedy creates organic reach beyond paid distribution.

#### 36. Clone Ads
Same person appears twice in the frame playing two roles â usually the "before" self and the "after" self, or the skeptic and the believer. Split screen or green screen effect.
Visual: Same creator on both sides. One is skeptical/struggling, the other is confident/transformed. They argue or react to each other.
Example: Left: "You're really going to spend $79 on green powder?" Right: "I spent $1,200 on supplements that didn't work. This replaced all of them."
Best for: Objection handling (the skeptic voice IS the audience's inner voice), before/after transformation, contrarian angles. The visual novelty of seeing the same person twice stops the scroll.

#### 37. Triple Clone Ads
Three versions of the same person debating or reacting. Adds a third perspective â usually the "neutral" or "surprised" version who reacts to the argument between the other two. More dynamic than clone ads, more shareable.
Visual: Three panels or three positions in frame. Each version has a distinct character â skeptic, believer, neutral witness.
Example: Skeptic: "AG1 is a scam." Believer: "I cancelled 8 subscriptions." Neutral: "Wait, show me the math on that."
Best for: Same as clone ads but with higher entertainment value. The third character creates a narrative arc. Best for cold traffic where you need maximum scroll-stopping power.

### Category D: Screen & Text-Driven

#### 38. Green Screen
Creator stands in front of a screenshot, article, review, or competitor's ad. The background IS the context.
Visual: Creator + screenshot of a Reddit post, a news article, a customer review.
Best for: Educational angles, myth-busting, reacting to customer language from the Pain Point Miner.

#### 39. Screen Recording
"Let me show you something..." â screen recording of a result, an order page, or a review.
Visual: Phone or desktop screen being navigated. Feels like proof, not production.
Best for: Result-showing, social proof, data-driven angles.

#### 40. Text over B-Roll
No voiceover, no talking head. Just text cards appearing over lifestyle or product B-roll footage. Each text card is a new piece of information or a new line of the hook. The viewer reads at their own pace. Pure visual storytelling.
Visual: Aesthetic B-roll (morning routine, product in use, lifestyle shots) with bold text cards appearing in sequence.
Example: B-roll of someone's morning â "I used to take 8 supplements" â B-roll of pill bottles â "Now I take one scoop" â B-roll of AG1 being mixed â "$2.63 a day"
Best for: Cold traffic, brands with strong visual assets, any product where the aesthetic sells. Works perfectly on mute (which is 85% of Meta video). Lower production barrier than talking-head content.

#### 41. Voiceover over B-Roll
Voice narrates while B-roll plays. No face on camera. The voice provides the story, the B-roll provides the proof or lifestyle aspiration. Feels documentary-style or cinematic depending on the B-roll quality.
Visual: Same B-roll approach as Text over B-Roll, but with a voice guiding the narrative.
Opening: "Here's something nobody tells you about greens powders..." or "I didn't believe it until I tried it myself."
Best for: Brands that want a premium feel, products with strong visual usage moments, storytelling angles. The faceless format means anyone can voice it â easier to produce than UGC. Can be repurposed across markets by changing the voiceover language.

#### 42. Fake Notification / Text Message
Mimics a phone notification, text conversation, or DM. Feels native to the platform.
Visual: iMessage bubbles, notification banner, Instagram DM screenshot.
Best for: Social proof (friend recommending), casual angles. Use carefully â overuse feels gimmicky.

#### 43. Whiteboard Explanatory
Creator draws or writes on a whiteboard (or digital equivalent) to explain a concept, break down ingredients, compare products, or illustrate a process. The progressive reveal of information IS the hook â each new thing written adds to the argument.
Visual: Hand drawing/writing on a white surface. Can be real whiteboard, glass board, or iPad. Camera shows the drawing appearing in real time.
Example: Creator writes "Your current stack:" and lists 8 supplements with prices â draws a line â writes "AG1:" with one price â circles the difference.
Best for: Education angles, comparison/value breakdown, ingredient analysis. The visual of someone building an argument in real time is surprisingly engaging. Appeals strongly to the Research-First Skeptic persona.

#### 44. Sticky Notes
Using physical sticky notes on a wall, table, or surface to build an argument, comparison, or ranking. Each sticky note is a new point. The physical act of placing notes creates movement and a progressive reveal.
Visual: Hand placing color-coded sticky notes. Each note has a short phrase. The wall fills up to reveal the full picture.
Example: Pink notes = "What I was taking" (one note per supplement). Green notes = "What AG1 covers" (matching each pink note). Visual comparison builds as notes accumulate.
Best for: Comparison angles, "before vs. after" stacks, ingredient breakdowns. The tactile, physical nature stands out in a feed full of digital content. Easy to produce â just needs sticky notes and a wall.

### Category E: Comparison & Ranking

#### 45. Split Screen
Before/after shown simultaneously. No need to wait for the "after" â the contrast is instant.
Visual: Left side = problem, right side = solution. Or: Day 1 vs. Day 30.
Best for: Transformation angles, result-driven hooks.

#### 46. Ranking Video
"I ranked every [product category] from worst to best." The hook is the implied verdict â the viewer wants to see where their product (or the advertised product) lands. Creates a curiosity loop that drives high hold rates.
Visual: Products laid out on a table or shown in sequence. Creator moves them into ranked positions. Numbers or tier labels on screen.
Opening: "I tried every greens powder on the market. Here's my ranking." or "Ranking the top 5 greens powders. Number 1 surprised me."
Best for: Cold traffic (the ranking format is native to social), competitive positioning, consideration-stage audiences. The product being advertised should rank #1 (obviously), but the format feels objective because it acknowledges competitors exist.

#### 47. Comparison List
Side-by-side comparison with a visual checklist. Feature-by-feature breakdown where one product clearly wins. Can be a person holding two products, a split screen with checkmarks, or a whiteboard comparison.
Visual: Two products side by side. Features listed. Checkmarks and X marks appear. The visual tally makes the winner obvious.
Example: "AG1 vs. my old supplement stack" â Price? Check vs. X. Convenience? Check vs. X. Third-party tested? Check vs. X.
Best for: Consideration-stage audiences comparing options, competitive positioning, value-for-money angles. The checklist format makes the argument feel logical rather than emotional â appeals to the Research-First Skeptic.

### Category F: Attention & Pattern Interrupt

#### 48. Pattern Interrupt
Something visually unexpected in the first frame. Breaks the feed pattern.
Visual: Product being smashed, liquid pouring in slow motion, unexpected color/movement.
Best for: Cold traffic, awareness. Pure attention-grabbing â needs a strong follow-up.

#### 49. POV Hook
"POV: [scenario]" â puts the viewer inside the experience.
Visual: Camera angle as if viewer is the one using the product.
Text overlay: "POV: You finally found a sunscreen that doesn't leave a white cast"
Best for: Aspirational angles, lifestyle positioning.

#### 50. Reaction
Creator reacting to something â their own results, a customer review, a competitor's claim.
Visual: Genuine surprise, excitement, or disbelief on the creator's face.
Best for: Social proof angles, UGC-style. The emotion on the face IS the hook.

#### 51. Listicle (Video Format)
Video version of the numbered list. "5 things I stopped buying after starting AG1." Each item gets a quick visual cut. The number in the hook creates a concrete expectation of value.
Visual: Quick cuts â one shot per list item. Number appears on screen for each. Fast pacing keeps attention.
Opening: "5 things I wish I knew before starting a greens powder." or "3 reasons I'll never go back to pills."
Best for: Educational angles, cold traffic. Odd numbers outperform even. The list format is native to social â people know what to expect and commit to watching.

### Framework Selection Notes

When matching angles to visual frameworks, consider these groupings:

**High hook rate, lower production effort:** Text over B-Roll, Voiceover over B-Roll, Screen Recording, Green Screen, Sticky Notes
**High hook rate, moderate production:** Clone Ads, Walking & Talking, Review, AMA, Comparison List
**High hook rate, higher production:** Skit Ads, Triple Clone, Interview on the Street, Stop Motion, Whiteboard
**Highest hold rate (people who stay, stay longer):** Yap + Founder Story, Ranking Video, ASMR + Unboxing, Visual Pacifier
**Most shareable (organic reach beyond paid):** Skit Ads, Interview on the Street, Triple Clone, Ranking Video

Every visual hook should be paired with a copy hook. The copy hook becomes the text overlay or voiceover opener. The visual framework determines HOW the message is delivered. Together they form the complete first 3 seconds.
